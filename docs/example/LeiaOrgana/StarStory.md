# STAR Story – Using Data to Drive Reliability Investments

## Situation

At Corellian Freight Analytics, engineering teams were spending a significant amount of time reacting to production issues, but there was little agreement about which problems deserved attention first. Every team had stories about painful incidents, and the loudest or most recent outage often became the next engineering priority. Product leaders wanted new features, Operations wanted greater stability, and engineering had more technical debt than capacity to address.

I became concerned that we were making investment decisions based on anecdotes instead of evidence.

## Task

My goal was to help Product, Operations, and Engineering develop a shared, objective understanding of where reliability work would have the greatest impact. Rather than asking teams to argue for their own priorities, I wanted everyone looking at the same data.

## Action

I partnered with Product and Operations leadership to identify a small set of reliability metrics that reflected actual customer experience rather than internal perceptions. We built dashboards that combined production incidents, service availability, deployment outcomes, support trends, and operational telemetry into a single view that leadership could review together.

Instead of beginning planning meetings by asking, "What feels broken?" we started by reviewing the metrics. We looked for recurring patterns, measured customer impact, and estimated the engineering effort required to address each issue. During discussions, I encouraged teams to treat anecdotes as useful hypotheses that still needed to be validated by data.

This shifted conversations away from defending individual priorities and toward collaborative problem solving. It also made it easier to explain why some popular requests were deferred while less visible infrastructure work became immediate priorities.

## Result

Over the following planning cycles, reliability investments became noticeably more focused and predictable. Teams spent less time debating priorities because decisions were backed by shared evidence. Several recurring production issues were addressed before they escalated into larger customer-facing incidents, and engineering leadership gained greater confidence that platform work was delivering measurable business value.

Perhaps more importantly, the culture began to change. Engineers, Product Managers, and Operations leaders started asking, "What do the metrics tell us?" before proposing solutions. That shift toward evidence-based decision making improved trust across teams and created a healthier balance between delivering new features and investing in long-term reliability.
