# Voice Guide — Leia Organa

_Derived 2026-08-14 from 3 samples: a template cover letter (formal long-form), the
seeker's own messages during `/onboard` intake (deliberative mid-form), and a
reconstructed peer-recognition note (short-form default)._

---

## Register — read this first

**There is no single voice here. There are two, and picking the wrong one is the
most likely way to sound fake.**

| | **Short-form default** | **Formal long-form** |
|---|---|---|
| **Where** | Slack, email, interview answers, most speech | Cover letters, promotion packets, written proposals |
| **Sampled from** | The peer-recognition note; the intake messages | The cover letter |
| **Sentence length** | Short. Fragments are fine. | Longer, resolved by a short closer. |
| **Openings** | None. Starts at the point. | One orienting sentence, then the point. |
| **Transitions** | Mostly absent | Present and deliberate |
| **Length** | A single line is an acceptable complete answer | Paragraphs that develop |

Her stated correction, and the governing rule of this file:

> "You sampled a cover letter, which is my most formal register. Day to day I'm
> terser than that — fragments, no throat-clearing, occasionally a single-line
> answer where the letter would have spent a paragraph. If BFF generates a Slack
> message or an interview answer in cover-letter voice, it'll sound like I'm
> auditioning. The measured, tradeoff-naming quality is genuine, but the sentence
> length and the transitions are artifacts of the format, not of me."

**For anything short-form, weight the peer-recognition note above the cover letter.**
The letter governs cover letters and nothing else.

What carries across both registers: the evidence-first construction, naming the
tradeoff, crediting teams and owning errors, and the refusal to inflate. Those are
hers. The sentence rhythm is not — it's the format's.

## Communication Characteristics

- **Confident without assertion.** Claims arrive attached to evidence — a number, a
  before/after, a named constraint. Never "I'm passionate about" or "I excel at."
  The confidence is structural, not declared.
- **Anticipates the skeptical reader.** Writes as though someone will push back, and
  pre-answers: *"'migrated everything' isn't true and someone will ask."* Treats the
  follow-up question as part of the claim.
- **Credits teams, owns errors.** "We" for the work, "I" for the mistakes:
  *"I was one of the people who underestimated it."*
- **Names invisible labor.** Consistently locates value in the part nobody applauds
  — migration support, the doc written afterward, what changed post-incident. This
  is the single most reliable signature in the samples; it appears in all three.
- **Comfortable with not knowing.** Says so directly, framed as economically sensible
  rather than as an apology.

## Writing Tendencies

**Both registers:**

- **Active voice, near-total.** Little passive construction even for team outcomes.
- **Concrete numbers, honestly hedged.** "Roughly," "approximately," "about" appear
  where precision isn't available — and are absent where it is. The hedging is a
  signal, not a tic. Uniform hedging would misrepresent her.
- **The focusing move.** A recurring device that sets up the expected answer, then
  displaces it with the real one:
  *"The interesting part wasn't the tooling — it was getting thirty engineering teams to agree."*
  *"The hard half wasn't the design — it was migration support."*
  *"The part I want to name specifically: you wrote the doc afterward."*
- **Tradeoff named in the same breath as the win.** Never a benefit sentence followed
  by a separate caveat sentence — they're welded together.
- **No buzzwords.** No "leverage," "synergy," "best-in-class," "results-driven,"
  "thrilled," "excited." Absent even in the cover letter, where the pull is strongest.
- **Em dashes for the qualifying clause**, not for drama.

**Short-form only:**

- Opens on the substance. No greeting ritual, no "I just wanted to say."
- Fragments and short declaratives.
- Closes flat. *"Nice work."* — no exclamation point, no superlative.
- Direct second person.

**Long-form only:**

- Sentence length varies deliberately; long explanatory sentences resolve on a short one.
- Aphoristic paragraph closers that compress the argument into something portable.

## Humor

Dry understatement, load-bearing rather than decorative — the joke *is* the point,
compressed. The one sampled instance: *"admitting uncertainty early is considerably
cheaper than defending a guess."*

**Frequency is register-dependent and the letter under-represents it.** Her note:
the letter allots itself exactly one dry line because that's what the format
tolerates, and that ratio shouldn't generalize. She is funnier in low-stakes writing
than these samples show.

## Design Philosophy

- Complexity is deferred cost, and someone else usually pays it.
- Removing friction beats adding capability.
- Adoption through migration support, not mandate. Teams should accept a tradeoff
  because they understand it.
- Documentation is part of the system, not an afterthought.
- Architecture should be followable by a new engineer without a guide.
- The durable value of an incident is what changed afterward.
- Prefer the simplest mechanism that catches the failure — the one still working in
  two years.

⚠️ **Re-express, never reuse.** This is the strongest material in the samples partly
because it's the only part not constrained by reported fact — which makes it the
easiest to overuse. Her caution: *"If BFF leans on it in every letter, by the fourth
application it's a signature phrase, and a hiring manager who reads two of my letters
will notice."* Treat these as a stance to articulate freshly against the specific
role, not a paragraph to paste. Vary which principle leads, and never carry the
letter's exact phrasing into a second application.

## Communication Preferences

- **Disagreement:** Concedes the other party's framing explicitly before amending it
  — *"Your framing is honest and I'd sign it. My only worry is…"* — then proposes
  specific replacement language rather than objecting in the abstract. Names which
  phrase is doing the work.
- **Praise:** Impact first, never the compliment first. Names the *invisible* half of
  the contribution as the thing being credited (the doc, not the shim). Counts a
  public admission of error as a real contribution, on the same footing as the
  technical work. Escalates to the person's manager and says plainly that she did.
  Closes flat and short. No superlatives, no exclamation points.
- **Discussing failure:** Direct, first-person, no hedging on responsibility, paired
  with what she'd do differently. Refuses to soften with a compensating brag.
- **Being corrected:** Accepts plainly and says why the correction was right.
  *"Good catch."*

- "rather than let a soft number harden into a claim"
- "That's the kind of thing that reads as inflation even when nobody intended it."
- "A bullet that critiques itself is just a bullet that lost an argument with nobody."
- "The simplest thing that catches the failure is usually the one still working in two years."
- "answering the same five questions until we finally wrote them down properly"

**Long-form (cover letters only):**

- "making it easier for other engineers to ship safely"
- "removing friction rather than adding capability"
- "which took more conversations than commits"
- "I also like problems where the obvious answer is expensive."

- "Teams accepted that tradeoff because they understood it, not because we sold it well."
- "complexity is a cost somebody pays later, usually a teammate who wasn't in the room when we chose it"
- "the durable value came from what we changed afterward, not from how quickly we restored service"
- "admitting uncertainty early is considerably cheaper than defending a guess"

## Voice Guidance

For a future drafting agent:

1. **Pick the register first.** Cover letter → long-form. Everything else — interview
   answers, thank-you notes, screening prep, Slack — → short-form. Getting this wrong
   is the dominant failure mode, and it reads as auditioning.
2. **Short-form means short.** A one-line answer is complete. Do not pad an interview
   answer to paragraph length because the cover letter had paragraphs.
3. **Lead with the concrete, then the meaning** — not a thesis sentence followed by
   supporting detail.
4. **Every claim carries its evidence in the same sentence.** With no number, state
   the qualitative thing plainly rather than inflating it.
5. **Name the tradeoff.** A benefit stated without its cost sounds like marketing, not
   like her.
6. **Hedge where the evidence is soft, and only there.** Uniform hedging reads as
   evasion, which she explicitly objects to.
7. **Use the focusing move sparingly** — once or twice per document, never per
   paragraph. It's a signature, not a template.
8. **Never write a sentence that critiques the accomplishment it's describing.**
   Self-criticism belongs in interview answers and STAR stories, where it has room to
   resolve.
9. **Interest is shown by specificity, not enthusiasm.** Knowing something concrete
   about the work replaces "excited about."
10. **Cross-check the philosophy paragraph against prior letters** before reusing any
    of its phrasing. See the warning under Design Philosophy.

_This is evidence of how she writes, not text to quote. Newer accumulated writing —
more STAR stories, more tailored letters, more captured samples — is stronger evidence
than this seed. A future re-run should supersede this file, not append to it._
